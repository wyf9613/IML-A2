\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Model Behaviour Analysis}

The test results show that Random Forest was the strongest model, with the lowest test RMSE of 1793.69 and the highest test $R^2$ of 0.9570.
This is likely because used car prices depend on non-linear relationships and feature interactions between brand, model, age, mileage, engine size, and other variables.
Random Forest can capture these patterns while using bagging to reduce the variance of individual trees, giving a better balance between flexibility and generalisation.

KNN also performed well, with a test RMSE of 1924.35 and test $R^2$ of 0.9505, but it showed a clear overfitting risk.
Its train RMSE was only 201.33, which is much lower than its test RMSE, suggesting that the model fitted local training samples too closely.
Ridge Regression was the weakest model, with a test RMSE of 2410.53 and train RMSE of 2410.12, indicating underfitting caused by its limited linear structure.
MLP learned non-linear patterns but did not outperform Random Forest, likely because tree-based models are better suited to this tabular dataset.
The single Decision Tree was also weaker because it had higher variance than the ensemble model.

\subsection{Error Analysis}

Larger prediction errors are more likely for high-priced luxury cars, rare models, and vehicles with extremely low or high mileage or engine size.
These cases are less common in the dataset, so the model has fewer similar examples to learn from.
Their prices may also depend more strongly on individual condition, special options, or market demand.

Rare models are especially difficult after one-hot encoding because each category may only have a small number of samples.
This makes it hard for the model to learn a stable pricing pattern for that specific model.
In addition, the outlier removal process helped stabilise modelling in the mainstream price range, but it may also limit generalisation to very high-end vehicles.

Some remaining error is also caused by missing information.
The dataset does not include variables such as location, trim level, accident history, maintenance condition, seller type, or listing time.
Listing price may also differ from the final sale price due to negotiation.
Therefore, not all prediction error should be treated as a model weakness.

\subsection{Preprocessing Impact}

Scaling had a strong effect on Ridge Regression and KNN because both models are sensitive to feature scale.
Without scaling, large-range variables such as mileage and tax can dominate the regularisation process or distance calculation, which leads to poor performance.

For Ridge Regression, the RMSE dropped from 7597.09 without scaling to 2444.76 with StandardScaler.
MinMaxScaler gave a very similar result, with an RMSE of 2442.11.
This shows that scaling is essential for regularised linear models in this dataset.

For KNN, the effect was even clearer.
The RMSE decreased from 6889.90 without scaling to 2097.92 with StandardScaler, while MinMaxScaler achieved 2415.64.
Since KNN defines similarity through distances, scaling is necessary to make the distance between cars meaningful.
The KNN scaling comparison was conducted on a tuning subset for runtime efficiency, so these values are mainly used to compare preprocessing choices.
Overall, preprocessing directly affected whether these models could learn useful patterns.

\subsection{Feature Importance Interpretation}

The Random Forest feature importance results show that the main predictors were \texttt{transmission\_Manual}, \texttt{year}, \texttt{engineSize}, \texttt{car\_age}, \texttt{mpg}, \texttt{mileage}, and \texttt{is\_luxury}, with importance scores of 0.3347, 0.2143, 0.1653, 0.0910, 0.0330, 0.0276, and 0.0273 respectively.
These features match common used-car pricing logic because they reflect depreciation, usage level, vehicle class, and brand premium.

\texttt{year} and \texttt{car\_age} represent depreciation, while \texttt{mileage} reflects how much the vehicle has been used.
Engine size and transmission are linked to vehicle specification and market positioning, and the \texttt{is\_luxury} indicator captures the price premium associated with luxury brands.
Ridge coefficients also showed that specific model identity has a strong predictive association with price, with positive coefficients for models such as Caravelle and negative coefficients for smaller economy models such as Up, KA, and Aygo.
These results should be interpreted as predictive associations rather than causal effects.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Improvements}

This study has several limitations.
First, the target variable is listing price rather than final transaction price, so it may include seller expectations, negotiation space, and pricing strategy.
Second, the dataset lacks important variables such as location, vehicle condition, accident history, trim level, seller type, and listing date.
These missing factors may explain part of the remaining prediction error.

Third, the data comes from the UK used-car market, so the findings should not be directly generalised to other countries or regions.
Market conditions, brand preferences, tax rules, and resale patterns may differ across locations.
There were also modelling limitations: cross-validation was only used as a robustness check on a 12{,}000-row subset due to runtime and course-scope constraints.

Future work could add more detailed vehicle and market information, then compare Random Forest with stronger tabular models such as Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, or LightGBM.
SHAP values or permutation importance could also be used to check whether the main predictors remain stable across model settings.
